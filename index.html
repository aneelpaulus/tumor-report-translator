Tumor Report Translator

Got a tumor DNA test report (sometimes called genomic, molecular, or next-generation sequencing testing, from labs like Foundation Medicine, Tempus, or Caris)? Paste it or upload the PDF to see each finding explained in plain English, along with FDA-approved drugs and recruiting clinical trials for that change.

Your report stays on your device. It is read inside your own browser. Only the gene names and changes found (for example "KRAS G12C") and the cancer type you choose are sent to public databases to look them up. Names, dates, and the rest of the report never leave this page.

This decides which drugs are shown as approved for this cancer. Pick "Other or not sure" if unsure.

PDF upload works for reports saved as digital PDFs (most patient portals). It can't read photos or scans; for those, type the findings in.

    Words you'll see

    Gene
    A set of instructions in DNA for making one protein. Cancer happens when changes in a few key genes make cells grow out of control.
    Mutation, variant, alteration
    A change in a gene. Reports use these words to mean much the same thing.
    Targeted therapy
    A drug designed to act on a specific change, such as one gene stuck in the "on" position.
    Variant of unknown significance (VUS)
    A change that scientists don't yet understand. It usually isn't used to choose treatment, and many turn out to be harmless.
    Tumor mutational burden (TMB)
    How many gene changes the tumor has. A high count (often 10 or more per megabase) can mean immunotherapy is more likely to help.
    Microsatellite instability (MSI)
    A sign that the tumor's DNA spell-checker is broken. MSI-High tumors often respond to immunotherapy.
    Variant allele fraction (VAF)
    The share of DNA in the sample that carries the change. Around 50% or 100% can sometimes hint that a change was inherited, though tumor tests can't say for sure.
    Somatic vs. germline (inherited)
    Somatic changes arose in the tumor and aren't passed to family. Germline changes are in every cell from birth and can be shared with relatives.

    This is not medical advice. It explains what a report's findings generally mean and what's publicly known about them. Treatment choices depend on the whole picture, which only the care team has. Bring these results and your questions to them.

    Where the information comes from. Gene explanations are written for this tool. Drug matches come from FDA drug labels via openFDA, trials from ClinicalTrials.gov, and research evidence (technical view) from CIViC, an open cancer-variant database. Findings are read from the report automatically and can be missed or misread, so check them against the report itself.

    Privacy. The report is processed in your browser. Only gene and change names and the cancer type are sent to openFDA, ClinicalTrials.gov, and CIViC. Nothing is stored. Not affiliated with any testing lab, the FDA, or any drug maker.
